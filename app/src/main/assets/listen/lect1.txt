Good afternoon, everyone. Today we will look at a pollutant that you cannot see but almost certainly carry: microplastics, pieces of plastic smaller than five millimeters. They come from two sources. Some are manufactured tiny, like the beads once used in facial scrubs. Others are fragments that break off bottles, bags, ropes and synthetic clothing whenever sunlight and friction wear them down.
Microplastics have been found in the deepest ocean trenches and on the highest mountains, in table salt, in drinking water, and even in the air of our homes. Ocean currents carry them across borders, which makes the problem impossible for any single country to solve alone.
Why should we care about particles so small? First, they enter the food chain. Shellfish and small fish mistake them for food, and the plastic moves upward as bigger animals eat smaller ones. Second, plastics carry chemical additives, and some of these chemicals disturb the hormone systems of animals. Third, tiny particles can be inhaled, and scientists are still studying what long term exposure means for human lungs.
The most frustrating fact is that microplastics are extremely difficult to clean up. A net that catches plastic bottles lets the particles drift straight through. Filters in washing machines can capture fibers from synthetic clothes, and a few countries now require them in new machines. But the cheapest solution remains prevention: design products that shed less, use less single use plastic, and improve recycling so that bottles become bottles again instead of litter.
Individuals are not powerless. Choosing a cotton bag, drinking tap water where it is safe, and washing clothes at lower temperatures all reduce the load. For your assignment this term, I want you to track your own plastic use for one week and calculate which single change would cut the most waste. You may be surprised by the answer.
