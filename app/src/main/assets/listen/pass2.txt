More and more cities are turning rooftops, abandoned railways and empty parking lots into green spaces. The trend is not merely about beauty. A growing body of evidence suggests that urban greenery is a form of public health infrastructure.
Doctors in some countries can now prescribe a walk in the park. Patients who spend two hours a week in natural settings report significantly lower levels of stress and better sleep. City trees also cool the streets in summer, absorb noise, and reduce air pollution, which benefits everyone, especially children and the elderly.
The benefits are social as well as physical. A well designed park invites strangers to share benches, watch children play, and exchange greetings. Sociologists found that people who live within a ten minute walk of a park know more of their neighbors and trust them more. In crowded cities, such daily contact quietly builds community.
However, green space is not distributed equally. Downtown areas, where land is expensive, often have the fewest parks, while the suburbs enjoy large lawns. This gap has a class dimension: residents of poorer neighborhoods suffer hotter summers and dirtier air, yet have fewer places to relax outdoors.
Planners are therefore testing creative solutions. Pocket parks squeeze greenery into the gaps between buildings. School playgrounds open to the public on weekends. Vertical forests climb up the walls of apartment towers. These projects cost far less than buying open land, and they bring nature to the doorstep of the people who need it most. The message for city leaders is clear. Green space is not a luxury to be added after everything else is finished; it is essential equipment for a healthy city.
